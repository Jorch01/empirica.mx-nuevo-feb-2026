GUIDE · PRESALES IN QUINTANA ROO

Presale condo not delivered in Cancún, Tulum or Playa del Carmen? Your rights and deadlines.

Your presale is late, stalled or shut down, and the developer won’t refund you. Mexican law gives you real tools — but two of them expire one year after you learn of the problem.

By Jorge Israel Clemente Marie, attorney licensed in Mexico (cédula profesional 08799163), managing partner at Empírica Legal Lab, Cancún. Legal review: pending.

If you paid a deposit on a pre-construction condo in Cancún, Tulum, Playa del Carmen or Puerto Morelos and the developer is late, has stopped building or will not refund you, Mexican law gives you real tools. But two of them expire one year after you learn of the problem. Act in months, not years.

First: what did you actually buy?

Most foreign buyers on the coast do not own anything yet. The coast is a “restricted zone” (Mexican Constitution, art. 27), so foreigners hold property through a bank trust (fideicomiso) approved by the Ministry of Foreign Affairs. In a presale you usually signed a promise agreement or joined the developer’s trust as a future beneficiary.

Until the deed is signed before a notary, you have a contract claim against the developer — not a title to the unit. That is why guarantees, deadlines and evidence matter so much.

The rules developers must follow in Quintana Roo

  • No presale without a guarantee. Since May 2026, state law allows presales only if the developer has a completion certificate, a bond in favor of the municipality, or a guarantee in favor of buyers. Sales without municipal authorization are void (Ley de Acciones Urbanísticas, art. 71).
  • Condo bonds. A condominium developer must post a bond for 15% of the project value to guarantee completion and another for 10% for quality (Condominium Law, art. 9).
  • Consumer protection applies to you. Mexico’s Federal Consumer Protection Law covers developers of homes and does not distinguish by nationality. The developer must give you its permits and licenses in a presale, register its contract with PROFECO, deliver on the date agreed, and cannot charge you before there is a written contract (arts. 73 Bis, 73 Ter, 74 and 75).
  • Penalties must be mutual. If your contract punishes only the buyer, that clause can be challenged (art. 73 Ter; NOM-247-SE-2021).
  • Licensed agents only. Real estate agents in Quintana Roo must hold a state license, and can lose it for keeping or diverting client money.

“Force majeure” is not an excuse for missing permits

Developers often invoke a grace period or caso fortuito (force majeure). A shutdown because the project lacked permits, or built more than it was allowed, is usually the developer’s own fault, not an unforeseeable event. Courts can also reduce a penalty that is excessive (Civil Code of Quintana Roo, art. 291).

Your four routes, in the order we usually use them

  1. Formal demand letter. A notarized or documented demand for delivery or refund, in Spanish, sets the record and starts the clock against the developer.
  2. PROFECO complaint. Free conciliation with the developer, available to non-residents. PROFECO can ask a judge to freeze the developer’s assets when delivery looks impossible, and the law sets a minimum compensation of 20% of the price paid. Deadline: one year.
  3. Civil or commercial lawsuit. For rescission, refund, penalties and damages. Slower, but enforceable against the company’s assets.
  4. Criminal complaint for fraud. The Quintana Roo Criminal Code specifically punishes sellers who divert buyers’ payments, or who take the price and neither deliver nor refund. If the developer pays you back, the case can end — which is exactly why it creates negotiating pressure. Deadline: one year from when you learned of the fraud.

The criminal route can take months just to identify the people responsible. That is why the written assessment and the formal demand letter come first: they protect your deadlines while the slower routes get going.

What to gather now

  • Your contract and every addendum, in the language you signed.
  • Proof of every payment (wire confirmations, not just receipts).
  • All emails and WhatsApp chats with the developer and the agent — export them before they disappear.
  • Any notice of delay, suspension, or a new price.
  • The developer’s and the trust bank’s legal names.

Mistakes we see every month

  • Signing a “termination agreement” that refunds you in installments with no guarantee — and then waiting.
  • Accepting a new, higher price to get the deed.
  • Wiring money to an agent’s personal account.
  • Waiting more than a year because the developer kept promising.

Can I do this from the United States or Canada?

Yes. Most steps can be handled with a power of attorney signed at a Mexican consulate, or when you visit Mexico. You do not need to move here to recover your money.
